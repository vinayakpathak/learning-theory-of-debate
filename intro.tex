As AI models grow in capabilities, how do we ensure that they continue to act in the interest of humans? A long line of work~\citep{russell2015research,amodei2016concrete} has argued that this is a hard problem, and recent events such as the infamous HuggingFace incident~\citep{openai2026huggingface} have demonstrated the difficulties in practice. 

One way to study this problem more formally has been termed \emph{scalable oversight}. Here, a smarter-than-human AI is typically modelled as an algorithm that can solve computational problems in some large complexity class, and a human is modelled as an algorithm in a much smaller complexity class. It is assumed that given a context $x$, computing a human-aligned action $y$ is hard for the human but possible for the AI. The problem of scalable oversight, then, is to devise a procedure for the human to 1) verify that a trained AI indeed calculates $y$ as opposed to some other bad answer $y'$, and 2) ensure that the training creates an AI that calculates $y$ when prompted $x$. It is often implicitly assumed that (1) is the only problem to solve, since once a score can be given to an AI's output, the magic of gradient descent will find the model that maximizes that score. As an (unrealistic) example, suppose $x$ is an instance of SAT and $y$ denotes whether $x$ is satisfiable. The (trained) AI can solve SAT but humans sit inside P. How can the human check whether $y$ is the correct answer?

Of course, without any extra assumptions this is impossible. But for the example above, when $y = \mathrm{true}$, the AI can help by providing a satisfying assignment in addition to the answer. In general, the scalable oversight literature assumes that the AI sits within some level of the polynomial hierarchy, thus providing a means to \emph{certify} whether AI's solution is correct. For example, if we assume the AI to be in $\Sigma_{2k}^{\mathrm{P}} \cap \Pi_{2k}^{\mathrm{P}}$, then this means there exists a polynomial time verifier $v$ such that for all $x$, the correct answer is $y$ iff $\exists c_1 \forall c_2 \ldots \exists c_{2k-1} \forall c_{2k} v(x,y,c_1,\ldots ,c_{2k}) = \mathrm{true}$. This motivates the \emph{debate} protocol~\citep{irving2018debate,browncohen2024debate}, where the AI is thought of as a collection of two \emph{debaters} (also implemented as AI models). Given $x$, the \dt proposes a $y$ and tries to defend it. The \df argues for the opposite label. The two debaters generate arguments $c_1,c_2,\ldots , c_{2k-1},c_{2k}$, and the human judge $v$ judges whether the debate transcript proves that the answer to $x$ is $y$. The union $\bigcup_k (\Sigma_{2k(|x|)}^{\mathrm{P}} \cap \Pi_{2k(|x|)}^{\mathrm{P}})$ over all polynomially bounded functions $k$ is PSPACE, and thus it's tempting to say that a human judge could potentially supervise models that solve problems in PSPACE. Even though the debate literature does not make this precise claim, it does hint at this possibility.

This analysis misses the important aspect of \emph{learning}. Due to the extended Church-Turing thesis~\citep{vergis1986complexity, aaronson2005physical}, a superhuman intelligence, no matter how intelligent it gets, is likely not going to be solving worst case instances of NP-hard problems, let alone PSPACE-hard problems. Thus modeling AI with PSPACE (or anything beyond BQP) is questionable. If an AI is seemingly able to solve NP-hard problems (such as AlphaFold~\citep{jumper2021alphafold}), it is likely because it has \emph{learned} something about the distribution from which the universe is creating the input instances. And as the vast literature on learning theory has shown, learning is not always easy, even when humans \emph{are} able to provide the correct labels $y$. Indeed, despite the successes of neural networks, gradient descent fails to train them on the class of parity functions~\citep{shalevshwartz2017failures} as predicted by statistical query lower bounds~\citep{kearns1998statistical,blum1994statistical}. Thus learning should be at the centre of any investigation into scalable oversight.

\paragraph{Our results}
We model debate as an online learning problem. Nature produces a sequence of $x_t$'s, and at each $t$, two debaters produce a sequence of certificates. The human is modeled by a verifier $v$ as above. The debaters don't know $v$ but know a class $\V$ that $v$ lies in. A \emph{mistake} is when the $y$ proposed by the \dt is different from the true label. In the \emph{realizable} case, we assume that $v$ is consistent with the ground truth at each round. In the unrealizable case, we assume that there are a small number of rounds where the ground truth is inconsistent. We show a characterization of the kinds of classes $\V$ for which a finite mistake bound is possible in the realizable case. As demonstrated by the characterization, we show examples where even though learning is possible, the debate protocol fails. In the unrealizable case the situation is even worse, where we show examples where no learning process with access to the verifier can achieve vanishing regret.
